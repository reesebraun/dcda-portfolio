Lab 4: Exploratory Data Analysis in Tableau

DCDA 40833 Skills Portfolio | Spring 2026

Visualization

This interactive map visualizes all recorded earthquakes with a magnitude of 6.0 or greater from 1900–2014, using data from the United States Geological Survey (USGS). Use the date filter and hover tooltips to explore patterns in earthquake location, magnitude, and depth over time.

Title: Map of Global Magnitude 6+ Earthquakes (1900–2014)
Source:United States Geological Survey (USGS)
Data Download: USGS Earthquake Data (Excel)

Reflection & Analysis

1. Data Preparation

I collected my dataset from Tableau Public’s sample datasets as an Excel file. I did not have any trouble downloading the file or creating a data source connection in Tableau. The dataset includes latitude and longitude coordinates, an earthquake ID, location descriptions provided by USGS, and associated attributes such as magnitude, depth, and gap.

One limitation I noticed during preparation was that the “place” field was repeated for earthquakes that occurred in the same general location, and locations were not broken down into standardized geographic categories such as country or region. This restricted my ability to filter or compare earthquakes by political or regional boundaries. Additionally, the dataset does not explicitly state the units used for magnitude, depth, or gap, which could limit interpretability and credibility for users unfamiliar with seismic data conventions.

I initially attempted to use a different dataset from Kaggle, but it lacked adequate geographic data such as latitude and longitude, which prevented making a meaningful map. I ultimately chose this dataset because of my background in environmental and geological sciences and my familiarity with spatial representations of earthquake data. I felt comfortable forming expectations about where earthquakes would occur and wanted to explore how magnitude, depth, and time interact spatially.

2. Design Choices

While this can lead to some overcrowding in high-activity regions, it allows viewers to compare shallow versus deep earthquakes within the same geographic area. I included a date filter so users can explore how earthquake patterns change over time, as well as a magnitude filter to focus on specific ranges of seismic intensity.

Tooltips include the location (“place”), date, magnitude, depth, and gap, which I considered the most relevant contextual information without overwhelming the viewer. Overall, my design choices aimed to balance clarity with interactivity while keeping the visualization focused on spatial exploration.

3. Expected Patterns

Based on my prior knowledge of plate tectonics, I expected the majority of earthquakes to cluster along tectonic plate boundaries, particularly around the Pacific Ring of Fire, including Japan, Indonesia, Alaska, and the western coasts of North and South America. I also expected to see linear patterns corresponding to subduction zones and mid-ocean ridges rather than a random global distribution.

The visualization strongly confirms these expectations. Earthquake activity is heavily concentrated along known plate boundaries, with dense clustering around the Pacific and along oceanic margins. I also expected deeper earthquakes to be more common in subduction zones, which is reflected in the larger, deeper points visible along the Pacific plate boundary. Viewing the map across the full date range further reinforces the idea that these tectonic regions remain consistently active over time, rather than experiencing isolated or random bursts of seismic activity.

4. Suprises & Outliers

One unexpected pattern was the relationship between depth and magnitude. While I initially assumed that deeper earthquakes might also be associated with higher magnitudes, the visualization suggests that many of the deepest earthquakes tend to have comparatively lower magnitudes within the dataset. This pattern stood out when comparing large point sizes (deep earthquakes) with their lighter color values.

Another subtle surprise was the presence of earthquakes that appear farther inland or slightly removed from the most visually obvious plate boundaries. While these events are fewer, their presence raises questions about intraplate seismicity or complex fault systems that are not immediately apparent from a simplified global map.

5. Questions for Further Investigation

  • Are deeper earthquakes generally associated with lower magnitudes or is this pattern specific to certain tectonic settings?
  • How much of the apparent increase in earthquake frequency over time reflects improved detection and reporting rather than actual increases in seismic activity?
  • Do inland earthquakes represent intraplate fault systems, or are they artifacts of how locations are labeled in the dataset?
  • How would the spatial patterns change if earthquakes below magnitude 6 were included?
  • Are certain regions experiencing clusters of earthquakes related to aftershock sequences following major events?

6. Tool Evaluation

Tableau Public is effective for creating clear, interactive visualizations without overwhelming users, particularly for maps, charts, and basic exploratory analysis. Its drag-and-drop interface makes it accessible for beginners and allows for rapid visualization when testing design choices. Features like interactive filters, tooltips, and easy publishing make it well-suited for exploratory storytelling and portfolio work.

However, Tableau has limitations compared to more specialized tools. Unlike ArcGIS, it does not support advanced spatial analysis such as buffering, proximity analysis, or spatial joins. Compared to Python or R, Tableau offers less flexibility for data cleaning, statistical analysis, and reproducibility. I also encountered limitations in customization and data manipulation, particularly when the dataset itself restricted how fields could be filtered or transformed. While calculated fields are available, they do not fully replace the control offered by code-based workflows.

Overall, Tableau Public works best as a visualization and exploration tool rather than a full analytical environment. It works well at making patterns visible and interactive but requires well-prepared data and is less transparent than scripted analysis when documenting each step of the process.
